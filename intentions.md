Pim Pam Pum has these intentions as a combat system:

- **Builds should be balanced across all team variations.** There are no fixed classes — players build characters by choosing skills and levels. Individual skill-vs-skill matchups can be lopsided (some skills naturally counter others, and that's intended). What matters is that no skill or combination is consistently worse than the others across team compositions of equal total skill level.
- **Balance is measured by total skill-sum parity.** A fight is fair when both teams have a similar sum of skill levels. The simulator's mirror matches (equal-budget random teams) should land near 50/50, and the web setup surfaces the team skill sum so a DM can balance encounters.
- All three action types (Atac, Defensa, Focus) should be good enough to see balanced play.
- No action should be so good that it's the best choice every time, nor so bad it never sees play.
- **Deliberate power tiers are fine, especially for objects.** Some cards and items are simply worse than others (a bastó is worse than a destral — and stays that way). Humble options are part of the fiction and the loot economy; parity is required between *skills* at equal level-sum, not between individual cards or items.
- **Armour is a small tuning lever, never a load-bearing one** (cuir 1, ferro 2 on the dice ladder since 2026-11-02: 2 and 3 stopped most level-1 blows outright). Passive armour should nudge a party's durability, not decide fights. The full range from no armour to a maximal loadout must not swing a combat's outcome by more than ~15 percentage points, and its effect must be even across enemy types — never a hard counter to one archetype (a flat per-hit subtraction silently becomes one against many-small-hits swarms, where it can negate almost everything). Encounter balance must not assume armour the player isn't guaranteed to have. Tune armour so it stays a light passive, not a switch. It need not pay off: whether to wear it is the player's call, and armour that is often the wrong call is acceptable (decided 2026-10-05, NEXT-STEPS §41). Asserted in `sets/fantasy/test/set.slow.test.ts`.
- Making good decisions should matter more than winning because of powerful actions.
- Anticipating others' plays should be greatly rewarded.
- Combos between actions played in the same round should be among the best options when set up right (allies can agree on combos beforehand).
- **The game should feel SWINGY, and swing should come from reads rather than from dice.** A round is a bet: everyone commits blind, and the reveal should pay out hard in both directions.
  - **Guessing right must feel like a save.** Playing a defense into the attack it turns out to intercept should visibly rescue someone — the difference between having guessed right and having guessed wrong is a big chunk of a character's health, not a rounding error.
  - **Catching someone undefended must feel brutal.** An attack that lands on a target with no defense up should take a large fraction of them off the table, not chip them. The punishment is what makes the guess matter.
  - **The intended shape is set up → execute.** Focus cards buff, mark or expose; the follow-up attack then hits so hard it can effectively remove an opponent in one blow. A big attack that lands unopposed after a turn of preparation is *supposed* to be a kill, and telegraphing that preparation is what gives the other side its chance to answer.
  - This is deliberately in tension with "prefer many smaller dice": tight dice make the *contest* predictable, which is what lets the READ, rather than the roll, carry the swing. Swing comes from the size of the payoff, not the variance of the roll.
- Combats should be dynamic, not stuck repeating the same move.
- **Combats should not go on for more than ~5 rounds.** This applies to all combats, including symmetric AI-vs-AI mirror matches — they may not stall longer either. A hero's PV (derived per level, never fixed: see below) and the dice on the cards are the main levers; tune them via simulation.
- **Priority, not speed (decided 2026-10-28).** A round resolves a fast step first (fast Defense → fast Attack → fast Focus), then Defense → Attack → Focus. Attacks aim at the reveal; defenses choose whom to guard after seeing the aims. Fast is a rare keyword, the exception that makes a card interesting (a lightning strike, a hide that beats the blow), never the bulk. There is no slow.
- **A correct read is never wasted, and never lost to information the player could not have had.** A rule or card that lets a right guess do nothing, or decides a round by something hidden rather than by the guess, is rejected however well it measures.
- **At the same skill level, a defense card should be stronger than an attack card.** Attacks always threaten (an undefended target takes the full roll); a defense only pays off when it intercepts, so the card itself gets a premium.
  - **The premium is big, not a nudge: a defense card should beat an attack card of the same level ~80% of the time.** Slightly larger dice is not enough — reading the attack correctly is supposed to shut it down, not shave a couple of points off it. The 80% is the target for the *contest itself* (defense total ≥ attack total, ties held by the defense); getting there is a dice job, and the gap can be closed with defense dice, attack dice or roll bonuses, whichever keeps the felt power curve intact.
  - **Read the 80% as the MEAN across same-level attacks, never as a floor against the worst of them.** This was calibrated the other way on 2026-08-08 — every defense sized so it beat the *strongest* same-level attack 80% of the time — which put the mean at ~90% and made a four-hero party of 85-90% guards effectively invulnerable: the kit analyzer's turtle test then had a defenses-only party beating a thinking one by up to 42pp. One die came back off every defense; the mean is 78% and the turtle collapsed. A guard that stops everything is not a premium, it is a win condition. Measured by `tools/src/experiment-defense-vs-attack.ts` — re-run it after touching any contest dice, and re-run the strategy-triangle test (`sets/fantasy/test/triangle.slow.test.ts`) after touching defenses at all.
  - **And a landed block should hurt.** Every defense card carries a payoff that fires when it blocks — damage back, a burn, a debuff, a heal, stone that soaks the next blow. Guessing right pays in more than damage avoided; it is what keeps Protect ahead of Aggro.
  - **Without Focus or buffs, a defense completely blocks an attack, on average** (the user, 2026-11-02). A correct read erases a vanilla attack: on the dice ladder one same-level defense lets about 0.5 PV through on average, and two defenses on one body nothing. Turtling is answered by making Focus stronger (the user, 2026-11-02: "why are you suggesting anything else than making focus stronger so that turtling is not right? isnt that what the intentions say?"): breaking or bypassing a defense is the job of Focus and buffs — an attack pumped enough to beat a same-level defense, a payload no guard stops, a stun, a guard-break. (Corrected 2026-11-05: this line used to read "defense is never softened to fix turtling (no graze, no smaller premium, no round clock)", the assistant's generalization, not the user's words; the user ruled on turtling, not on other problems such as fight length.) The direction (2026-11-02): each kit's first Focus card beats a turtle. If turtling still cannot lose once Power can punish it, the attack-against-defense dynamic itself is reconsidered. **A defense covers one body** (the user, 2026-11-02): guarding an ally leaves the defender open, so a guarding pair can no longer wall both bodies at once. Measured that day (2v2 mirrors, 300 fights a cell): a pure defender's stalls against the AI fell from 69% to 47% at level 6 and from 36% to 29% at level 10, and the triangle at level 6 came closest to balanced (Power vs Protect 47, Protect vs Aggro 50); level 3 did not move, because there even AI-against-AI fights stall a third of the time: its blows barely scratch 26 PV (the PV calibration's question).
- **Prefer many smaller dice over fewer bigger dice** (e.g. 2d4 over 1d8): tighter distributions make contests less swingy. Big single dice (1d12) are reserved as deliberate swingy flavour for wild skills.
- **Dice are the felt power curve, and 1d4 is the unit** (the user, 2026-11-02). Every number is built up from the smallest die: a level-1 attack or defense rolls about 1d4, and higher levels roll bigger dice (the ladder below). Players should experience progression as lower dice → higher dice. PV is derived from the same ladder, never the other way round.
- **Simplicity over mathematical complexity.** Whenever possible prefer flat modifiers and plain dice over formulas — lower the players' cognitive load at the table. A card should read as "arma +2" or "2d6", not as a computation.
- **Fatigue is the DM's pressure dial, and it is a dice modifier — on the players only.** Five discrete levels (Cansat → Esgotat), each −1 on every roll, assigned from the fiction and cleared only by a 4h+ rest. Playing cards costs no fatigue — except a card whose text says so, where the exhaustion is the price of a heroic act (Entrar en Fúria: the berserkergang's crash; unplayable at Esgotat, which also bounds re-entering it). Measured 2026-09-13: one level is worth roughly one difficulty tier (a 65% fight is 49% at level 1, 31% at level 2, 6% at level 5), so a level is handed out like a tier, never as flavour; the real decision it creates is *push on or spend four hours*.
  - **Enemies never carry fatigue.** The 2026-07-17 rejection of roll penalties ("they freeze fights") measured them *symmetrically*, accruing inside a mirror match: since damage is the margin, −X on both sides cancels and only the min-0 floor survives, which doubles fight length (3.9 → 7.7 rounds at −5, re-measured). One-sided, the same penalty *shortens* fights (5.0 → 4.2), because the tired side also defends worse. The rejection stands for symmetric penalties; a player-side dial is a different mechanism.
  - **All rolls, not just attacks.** Attack-only penalties cost the same winrate but lengthen fights (the freeze direction); defense-only penalties are nearly free (−5 costs ~2pp, defenses win ~80% of contests and undefended auto-hits never roll one). The all-rolls scope is the only one that bites *and* keeps fights short — and it is the simplest rule.
  - Pacing across a day now comes from the dial, not a budget. The cards the old budget priced above 1 (the "esgotadora" cards, listed in `NEXT-STEPS.md` §11) are unpriced until their replacement cost is decided.

## Strategy Triangle

The system aims for a **Power > Protect > Aggro** triangle, now expressed through the skill mechanics:

- **Power** (focus-heavy play) beats **Protect** (defense-heavy play) — powerful buffs/transformations eventually overwhelm pure defense, which doesn't advance the win condition.
- **Protect** (defense-heavy play) beats **Aggro** (attack-heavy play) — defenses resolve before attacks and are stronger at equal level, so their dice reliably absorb the attack margin; the defender eats penetrating hits so the protected ally stays safe, and every defense card pays off when it blocks.
- **Aggro** (attack-heavy play) beats **Power** (focus-heavy play) — focus actions resolve after attacks, so any attack that lands interrupts an undefended focus before it resolves.

This emerges because the strongest focus actions are **high risk / high reward**: very powerful but resolving last, so the best play is often protecting an ally who is committing to a big focus. A few fast focus actions (dodges, small buffs) add variety without demanding full team protection. A focus that pumps an attack enough lets that attack break through a defense — the prepared attacker who sweeps is a pattern the game wants.

**When a corner of the triangle dominates, BUFF THE CORNER THAT BEATS IT.** The
reflex is to nerf whatever is winning; the rule here is the opposite. If Protect
is dominating — the measurable symptom is the strategy-triangle test
(requirement 4) failing on Power > Protect — diagnose why, then **buff Focus
cards until the balance returns**, rather than shaving defenses down. Power is
supposed to beat Protect, so Protect running away means Power is too weak, and
cutting Protect would only lower the whole game's power level instead of
restoring the relationship. The same applies around the triangle.

A note on what actually answers a turtle, since not every focus does: buffing an
attack is useless against a wall of guards that holds 85% of everything. The
cards that break a turtle are the ones a guard cannot stop — undefendable
payloads (Guèiser, Riu de lava), reaps that ignore defenses (Mà de la tomba),
and build-ups big enough that no guard survives what they eventually land.
Enemy kits are especially thin here, which is why a turtling party currently
goes unpunished.

Balance levers that maintain the triangle (being tuned via simulation):
- **Focus power** — a resolved high-impact focus should be game-changing (large skill buffs, transformations). **Focus cards are much stronger than attacks of their level** (the user, 2026-11-02): a Focus is not guaranteed to be played — it is slow and breaks when its player is hit first (Aggro's answer) — so it must be that much stronger than a similar attack, and when one lands it blasts through defenses. The picture is Pokémon's Swords Dance: one good setup, one good Focus combo, and the whole enemy team is gone. Every bender Focus was scaled up that day — buffs and payloads (Foc interior +10, Túnel opens every attack of the group, Trampa de gel freezes two), and the protective ones too (bigger heals, a thicker fog, +6 armour). A Focus that rolls may sit up to 6 above its level's attack (each set's `test/ladder.test.ts`).
- **Fast cards** — how many there are, and of which type: fast attacks land before normal defenses, fast focuses before normal attacks.
- **Defense payoffs** — what a block pays when it holds.
- **Attack dice vs defense dice and armour** — damage is the margin, so small unbuffed attacks should struggle to get through a defense plus armour; buffs and coordination make them land.
- **PV pools** — low enough that clean hits threaten characters and combats end in time.

## Skill design intentions

- Each skill should have a clear identity, lore, and a unique combination of strengths and weaknesses; actions should feel great and make sense for the skill's theme.
- Each skill should have at least one **signature action** with a distinctive, high-impact effect that breaks the normal action mold, at level 6 or below (players live at levels 4-6, below).
- Actions unlock at increasing skill levels, so a high-level skill rewards investment with stronger/rarer actions. **The ordinal IS a power ramp: within any skill — player or enemy — later actions should be stronger, and a higher-level character should definitely be stronger than a lower-level one.** Enemy kits order weakest attack at level 1 and signature power late, so a fielded enemy's level is a genuine difficulty lever.
- **The dice ladder** (2026-11-02, the user; `sets/common/src/ladder.ts`). A card of level L averages about L + 1.5: 1d4 at level 1, then 1d6, 2d4, 2d4+1, 2d6, 3d4, 3d4+1, 4d4, 3d6 and 3d6+1 at level 10 (one die type and a flat bonus, many small dice before one big one). A defense of level L holds against that level's attack ~78% of the time: 1d6, 2d4, 2d6, 3d4, 2d6+2, 3d4+2, 2d8+2, 5d4, 4d6, 4d6+1 (the defense premium above). Effects, extra targets and speed are paid for in dice; a card rolls above its rung by more than a point only when something else pays (fatigue, felling its own player, cancelling the side's attacks: `test/ladder.test.ts` in each set). Effects counted in PV were doubled with PV (Cremat 2, Anell de foc 6, Parany de molla 6).
- **Fights are short: no more than 6 rounds, at every level — a hard limit** (the user, 2026-11-02). PV, dice and cards answer to it. Measured that day with every hero on one fixed PV (2v2 hero mirrors, the AI): fights that end take about 12-13 rounds at levels 3-9 and 3-4 at level 10, and some kit pairs (water and earth with each other, the shared kits) deadlock to the 40-round cap.
- **A hero's PV is never fixed: it is derived, per level, from the requirements** (the user, 2026-11-06). Damage grows with the ladder, so a single value makes low-level fights drag and high-level fights burst. A hero's PV is a base plus what each level in each of its classes adds (the user, 2026-11-06): bulky classes add more and fragile ones less, in the designer's order of the classes, which gives the cast bulk diversity and makes PV a balance lever; a multi-class hero adds every class's share. Lower levels carry a little less than higher ones, and as low as interactivity allows if fights would otherwise run long. Each set derives the numbers with `tools/src/derive-pv.ts` (fights end in time; no knockout without an answer; each class toward an even fight with the field) and pins them; re-derive them whenever the ladder, the structure or a set's cards move far. A creature's PV is not fixed either (the user, 2026-11-03): with its level it is one of an encounter's two main levers; the DM picks which creatures and how many.
- **Interactivity matters: nobody dies to something they could do nothing to stop — at any level** (the user, 2026-11-06). Swing from reads is welcome, and it grows with level: by level 10 players read well, and a wrong read may be punished hard. A death no read could have prevented is a bug at every level, level 10 included. A fresh hero felled in one play dies without an answer when the card was **fast** (it resolves before every normal defense and attack), when the damage was **uncontested** (a PV loss with no roll against a guard: guarding does not stop it, and an attack cannot be interrupted), or when it went **through their own guard** (they read the attack right and still went from full to 0). A slow attack on a hero who did not guard, a slow Focus (hitting its caster first interrupts it), a counter and a round-end status all had an answer. (Llamp went from a fast 4d6, a 76% one-shot, to 2d6 for this. It replaces "a fast attack is weak, or something answers it" and its widening to every card, "a fresh hero survives one blow".)
- **Heroes start at level 3 in their main skill** (the user, 2026-11-02), so every hand holds an attack, a defense and a Focus from the first fight. The triangle needs all three: at levels 1-2 a hand holds an attack and a defense only, nothing punishes a defender, and the exact referee found always-defending nearly optimal there (1.1 PV lost over two rounds against the best play, level 2, 8 positions). So the play that matters, and what measurement covers, is levels 3-10; each kit's level-3 card is its first Focus, the one every hero starts with.
- **Players live at levels 4-6; levels 7-10 are finishers** (the user, 2026-11-02). A class is fully playable by level 6: its variety, its heals and utilities and its signature moves all sit in levels 1-6, so nobody waits for level 7 to play the class. Levels 7-10 hold only amazing finishers, the kit's biggest payoffs, a reward for investment and never a utility the class needs. The power ramp still holds (a finisher outclasses everything below it); what changes is that no card the class needs in order to work sits above level 6.
- **Neighbouring cards are never too similar** (the user, 2026-11-02). Two cards at adjacent levels of a kit do clearly different jobs: the next level opens a new option, never a slightly different copy of the last one. Tempesta d'espurnes and Alè de drac, two multi-target fire attacks at levels 4 and 5, were too close.
- **Low levels must already offer diverse options.** By level 2-3 a player should be choosing between action TYPES the skill owns (an attack AND a defense/focus), not locked into one move because they aren't "high enough". The ramp orders power *within* each type; it never front-loads one type exclusively. (Kits that deliberately lack a type — the all-attack Berserk, support kits with no attacks — are exempt for the types they don't have.)
- Draw inspiration from D&D and other RPG skills/abilities; design actions around memorable moments.
- New actions should be original (not reskins of existing ones) and balanced against the existing set.

## Out-of-combat challenges

- **`1d20 + the action's dice` vs a DM difficulty**, meet-or-beat. Card dice alone can't carry these checks: they're calibrated to contest *other cards*, both sides around 7, and a world difficulty is not another card. The d20 sets the scale; the card is what training buys on it. A level adds nothing to a roll, here or in a fight (2026-11-01): a skill helps through the cards it knows.
- **Anyone may attempt anything** — no fitting skill, bare d20 (~55% at difficulty 10, 5% at 20, never at 25). A skill is not permission to try, it's what makes you good. Hence no Perception or Investigation skill, ever.
- **The margin is the payload**, as in combat. Never a bare yes/no.
- **Failing by 1 or 2 teaches, including from zero** — the one case a fight can't produce, since in combat you only play cards you know.
- **Reward invention by lowering the difficulty**, never with a bonus: nothing to price, nothing to argue about.

> When simulation results conflict with these intentions, flag the discrepancy and propose fixes.
